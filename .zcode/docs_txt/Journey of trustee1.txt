Journey of Trustee
Becoming Managing Trustee of the Appropriate Institute of Rural Development (AIRD) is not merely accepting a position in an organization; it is accepting a lifelong responsibility to serve society with honesty, compassion, and dedication. The journey of a trustee is a journey of continuous learning, self-development, and collective action for the welfare of rural communities.
A trustee begins with a simple belief that every human being deserves equal opportunity to live with dignity. Inspired by the ideals of Gram Swaraj envisioned by Mahatma Gandhi, the trustee works to strengthen people's participation in local governance and to promote transparency, accountability, and sustainable development.
The journey starts with understanding village life. A trustee visits villages, interacts with women, youth, farmers, elected representatives, and disadvantaged families. Through listening rather than instructing, the trustee learns about local strengths, problems, aspirations, and available resources. This understanding becomes the foundation for planning meaningful development initiatives.
The trustee believes that real development cannot be imposed from outside. It must emerge from within the community. Therefore, AIRD promotes a participatory approach, where villagers identify their own priorities, prepare action plans, and work collectively with government departments, banks, and corporate institutions to solve local problems.
One of the important responsibilities of a trustee is to guide the establishment of a live model village. In this model, women Self-Help Groups (SHGs), Gram Panchayat members, village youth, and local institutions work together to improve governance, strengthen livelihoods, and manage local resources effectively. The trustee encourages youth to become Change Agents (CAs) through practical training, enabling them to continue the development process within their own communities.
The journey is not without challenges. Limited financial resources, inadequate community participation, and administrative constraints often slow progress. Instead of becoming discouraged, a trustee treats every difficulty as an opportunity to learn and improve strategies. Every success and every failure becomes a lesson for designing more effective interventions.
The trustee also believes that development is incomplete without spiritual and moral values. Inspired by the teachings of Swami Vivekananda, Gautama Buddha, and other great thinkers, AIRD encourages meditation, self-discipline, honesty, compassion, and service to humanity. Development is viewed not only as economic progress but also as the growth of character, responsibility, and social harmony.
Transparency is another guiding principle of the trustee's journey. AIRD promotes documentation, community participation, social audits, and digital information systems to ensure that development activities remain open, accountable, and accessible to everyone.
As the years pass, the trustee's role evolves from being a learner to becoming a mentor. Experience gained through fieldwork is shared with students, researchers, development professionals, government officials, and voluntary organizations. The aim is to replicate successful participatory models in a village and inspire more people to work for rural transformation.
Ultimately, the journey of a trustee is measured not by wealth or recognition but by the positive change created in the lives of people. It is a journey of service, humility, and hope—a journey dedicated to transforming villages into self-reliant communities where every person has the opportunity to participate in development and enjoy a life of dignity.

I was born in 1958. As a child, I was innocent by nature but mischievous in mind. I was good in studies, so everyone loved me. In 1974, when I was studying in Class 9, my father passed away. After that, I became even more mischievous and drifted away from studies. By the time I regained awareness of my responsibilities, I had already failed in high school. Then I decided that I wanted to become a good human being, and I devoted myself seriously to studies.
My father practiced meditation. In order to learn meditation, he had even stayed in forests with Neem Karoli Baba. Once, Baba also visited our home. My father had a spiritual guru, but I did not. Therefore, I began reading the literature of Swami Vivekananda. Through this, I understood the deeper causes and purposes of life. I also studied the teachings of Gautam Buddha. In 1977, Shri Jitendra Bahadur Seth, a disciple of Maharishi Mahesh Yogi, taught me meditation, and I began practicing it. In 1981, at Maharishi Mahesh Yogi’s Rishikesh Ashram, I learned deep meditation from Shri Rajendra Mishra.
In 1982, after completing my studies in Economics, my classmate Himanshu was trying for the post of Research Assistant under a village study project at the Appropriate Technology Development Association (ATDA), located in Gandhi Bhavan. After being repeatedly called for interviews, he was asked to come again. I requested to accompany him. He said, “You may come along, but don’t enter the office.” So I waited at a tea stall near the gate while he attended the interview.
When he returned and told me he had been called again for the day after tomorrow, I said, “Now you wait here, I will go inside.” Since our qualifications were equal, I wrote an application and entered the office. After nearly an hour-long interview, I was told to join work immediately. I explained that I had not come seeking employment but to understand why Himanshu, who was qualified in every way, was not being selected. I also wanted to know why he, who had always performed better than me academically, was repeatedly called while I was offered immediate appointment. I clarified my thoughts and said that I would discuss the matter and salary of Rs. 450 per month with my family and inform them later.
Shri Varun Vidyarthi told me that he practiced meditation and that during meditation he had sensed that someone like me was coming.
For Himanshu this was surprising, and for my family it seemed madness. But to me, the work being done by ATDA appeared to be a great opportunity—especially the chance to learn Participatory Action Research (PAR) from Varun Vidyarthi. Somehow I obtained permission from my elder brother to work there for six months. That six-month permission kept extending and, after many years, my commitment was declared an incurable madness. That gave me peace because now I could do whatever I truly wished to do.
I began spending much of my time in the village of Garhi. Villagers benefited from my services, and I received their blessings. ATDA kept giving me opportunities, and I continued developing participatory management approaches in different sectors, gradually becoming skilled in working on development processes. My meditation practice also became increasingly fruitful, and I felt I was beginning to move toward a state of higher wisdom.
In 1984, I received an opportunity to conduct Participatory Action Research in the villages of Almora regarding the role of local people in forest management. In this project, a sharp and capable woman named Neera Kool was appointed. Three years later, she became my life partner because she too, like me, had resolved never to marry.
In 1986, after the research work was completed, she still wished to continue working with me even without any formal project. Her companionship had become essential for my work. I delegated much of the work to her. She was highly capable, and I also needed a woman colleague to work effectively among men in villages. To prevent society from objecting to our living and working together, we somehow convinced our families and got married. Our families believed that marriage would eventually “normalize” us. But nothing really changed—we continued the same way of life and work.
On the very second day after marriage, I was in Nainital organizing a workshop with Dr. Omkar Upadhyay for teachers to determine educational material and processes. Together, Neera and I began working on research related to rural development processes. We continued receiving blessings for our efforts.
In 1989, under a Government of India scheme and in collaboration with the Centre for Environmental Education, Ahmedabad, I initiated environmental education in government schools of Uttar Pradesh. With support from the Ministry of Human Resource Development, ATDA gave me the opportunity to conduct PAR on “How teachers can disseminate environmental education.” Through ATDA, I also coordinated the work of the Regional Resource Agency at the divisional level for the Ministry of Environment’s environmental awareness programmes.
In 1990, we were blessed with a son. At that time both Neera and I were working at ATDA and contributing to development activities. ATDA had first given me my work, then my life partner, and through meaningful work we received a healthy son. I felt deeply blessed, and my dedication to development work intensified further. I conducted PAR on reclaiming saline wastelands and helped develop barren lands in many villages.
My work and working methods also impressed India Development Group UK Ltd. Through ATDA, I received opportunities to facilitate study programmes for students from universities in several countries regarding rural development conditions and suitable participatory processes.
Through ATDA, I also contributed to various movements organized for protecting the rights of rural people. I was satisfied with my work and deeply grateful to Shri M. M. Hoda for giving me opportunities to do what I truly wanted. Hoda Sahib wanted me to go to London for further studies in development processes, but I replied that I had not yet fully utilized what I had already learned, so what would be the use of studying more? The course intended for me was then given to Shri B. K. Singh. Hoda Sahib was upset, but our relationship continued to deepen, and I came to regard him as my adoptive father.
We were both happy with our work and achievements. Due to our good deeds, in 1995 we were blessed with another son. We felt fortunate and continued working joyfully.
Unfortunately, Hoda Sahib developed Parkinson’s disease. At that time research for treatment was ongoing, and he donated his body to a London institution for research purposes. He began living in London with his wife.
ATDA conducted research on several production units, including research on establishing village-level units. One such unit was a cement production unit established in Mohanlalganj. The research work for all units was successful, and cement production had even begun.
After completion of the research phase, Shri Jitendra Sharma was appointed manager of the cement production unit. Sharma Ji was also in charge of several other units. Due to inefficient management, the cement unit continued incurring losses and eventually had to be shut down. Employees could not even be paid salaries. After waiting for two years, the employees’ agitation intensified greatly. Once, when Hoda Sahib had briefly returned to Lucknow, the employees surrounded his house demanding immediate payment. After much persuasion from my side, the protest calmed down.
There were three officers in ATDA who had originally been entrusted with the responsibility of selling the machinery and paying the dues of employees and government agencies, but they had failed to do so. Seeing the seriousness of the situation, it was decided that I should take responsibility. Hoda Sahib appointed me as Executive Officer of the ATDA and instructed me to sell the machinery as scrap and make payments to employees at the earliest. The employees then lifted the siege of his house. To free my adoptive father from distress, I accepted this responsibility, though I had never been connected with any production unit before. I was authorized to sell the machinery for up to Rs. 17 lakhs.
Now the employees began surrounding me instead. At that time I was facilitating research on Panchayati Raj for a woman from London. The employees made it difficult for me to work. Somehow the scrap dealers also learned of the situation and began visiting the office. Many firms inspected the machinery and submitted quotations. The quotations were opened on the scheduled date. Since the highest quotation—Rs. 19.25 lakhs—was submitted by the firm of Kamal Dhawan and Malik, they were awarded the contract to dismantle the machinery. The first cheque of Rs. 5 lakhs received from the firm was deposited into ATDA’s account.
The machines began to be dismantled and loaded onto trucks. Employees had surrounded the unit and declared that no truck would leave until their dues were paid.
J. Sharma became afraid of me because he was in charge of several units. He feared that after reviewing the cement factory I might also review the accounts and functioning of other units under him. Therefore, Sharma Ji turned all employees and governing members of the ATDA against me. The Council of Science & Technology (CST) had provided the cement factory to ATDA on the condition that ATDA would conduct research and, if ever sold, it would only be sold back to CST. Sharma Ji complained against me to CST Director Pathak Ji. CST began preparing to file an FIR against me. Kamal Dhawan, however, played a decisive role in neutralizing the proceedings against me.
Since I was unable to obtain funds from ATDA to pay employee dues, employees continued stopping the trucks. The situation kept worsening. Finally, I obtained conditional cash support from Kamal Dhawan and, with the cooperation of ATDA’s Accounts Supervisor A. K. Verma and Cashier Rastogi, made cash payments to employees so the trucks could leave. The condition of taking cash from Kamal Dhawan was that the money would be returned once ATDA received the cheque payment. Unfortunately, the cheque of Rs. 5 lakhs could not be encashed.
To pay salaries of ATDA office staff, I again had to take conditional cash support from Kamal Dhawan. Since I was the senior-most officer of ATDA, paying staff salaries was also considered my responsibility. I believed that the cheque deposited in ATDA’s account would eventually clear and the borrowed money could be returned. But the cheque never materialized. Government notices regarding taxes and dues also began arriving, and these too were paid with Kamal Dhawan’s assistance. Kamal Dhawan wanted to clear ATDA’s liabilities and take away all the machinery, and since funds were not forthcoming from ATDA, bank pay orders were arranged and employee dues paid.
Sharma Ji continuously tried to ensure that all governing body members turned against me. At his insistence, the Accounts Supervisor, with help from the cashier, even removed receipts related to cash payments. As a result, the complete accounts of expenditures made by me could not be traced. In the hardest time of my life Col. Man Singh helped me to save from the web of Sharma Ji, but I was declared dishonest
At that difficult time, Shri Shiv Gopal Mishra, a member of ATDA’s governing body, a true trade union leader associated with several railway unions, stood firmly in my defense. Because of his efforts, severe action against me was avoided. However, the governing body removed me from my position and appointed Sharma Ji in my place. Sharma Ji then completed the remaining sale processes initiated by me. ATDA no longer had any place for me. What Sharma Ji later did was no longer my concern. He was capable and knew how to manage people, so he began selling not only the cement unit but also machinery and materials from ATDA’s other units.

Hoda Sahib still had complete faith in me. Through the India Development Group India Chapter, he gave me an opportunity to prepare a project proposal for submission to the National Lottery Charity Board, London. Judith Crossland and I had already become friends through earlier projects, and our relationship deepened further. She held an important position in the London office, while I was responsible for work in the India Chapter. Fortunately, a project worth Rs. 1.5 crores was approved. I felt that my honesty had finally borne fruit.
My head office now became India Development Group, London. The India Development Group (India Chapter) office continued to function from ATDA premises, while I shifted my office to the officers’ bungalow of the cement factory. ATDA no longer had anyone capable of bringing in projects and funding. I continued sitting in the ATDA office and paid rent to help sustain it.
In this project, Dr. Bhadauria was appointed Director of the India Development Group (India Chapter). Unfortunately, he did not prove honest and failed to implement the project according to the plan. I continued sending reports of the project activities to London. Based on my reports, action was taken and, after one year, financial assistance to the project was suspended.
At a workshop in Holland, I was honored as an expert in participatory management. I cried deeply because I felt that despite all my efforts, I had still not succeeded in creating a truly successful village-level model. Judith later explained to me in London that my failures were due to the fact that I was working in the name of service while still functioning within the framework of a paid job.
India Development Group arranged a meeting for me with the National Lottery Charity Board. In the meeting held in London, there was extensive discussion on the causes of failure and possible solutions. After the meeting, financial support for the project resumed. For about two years the work progressed well. But then M. M. Hoda, who headed both ATDA and IDG, passed away.
After his death, leadership responsibilities were being considered for me, but Dr. Giri was appointed head of IDG because he was much more qualified than I was. Comrade Shiv Gopal Mishra became head of ATDA. Dr. Giri and Dr. Bhadauria together began misusing funds and resources, so I resigned from my position in IDG.
Akhilesh Tiwari, who had worked with me very honestly in ATDA, was running an organization called SDF but struggling to sustain it. Therefore, I began supporting SDF. I also wanted to gain experience working with Self Help Groups. Together with NABARD and UNICEF, I worked on SHGs and helped strengthen the organization. Judith also arranged financial support for SDF through one of her friends. He was named Bank Uncle because he wanted to remain an unknown person. Unfortunately, Akhilesh too became greedy for money.
Therefore, I decided to work voluntarily and without any project in Gosai Purwa, a hamlet under Banarki Gram Sabha in Suratganj Block of Barabanki district. I knew no one in the village. I ate wherever someone offered food and slept wherever I found space. Villagers could not understand my way of living and interacting with people, so they asked Gram Pradhan Anand Singh to remove me from the village. However, after discussions with him, arrangements were instead made for my food and stay at his home. The Pradhan appreciated my work and extended full support. At that time I was President of Lions Club Lucknow Maitri, therefore, many Lions Club members visited the village and supported my work. The Pradhan liked the Lions Club and later became a member himself. I worked with youth to learn participatory management process to develop agriculture and educate children. I consider it a great success of my working. 
After about three years, when I returned back to Lucknow after achieved success in participatory management processes, I found ATDA in a very poor condition. Sharma Ji had sold all the units. Nothing remained with ATDA except its office in Gandhi Bhavan. Seeing the condition of the institution from where I had begun my work saddened me, so I again tried to support it. Shri Shiv Gopal Mishra appointed me as Project Director.
At ATDA, I worked with students of Social work from Lucknow University to promote participatory development processes. Under an OXFAM project, I also secured financial support for developing five mobile libraries. With the help of Mr. S. K. Rehman, I built a mobile library rickshaw. I requested authority for empowering me to withdraw funds for the project but did not receive it, making the work difficult. Therefore, after building one library rickshaw, I left the project.
At that time Dr. S. C. Trivedi invited me to work as CEO of his organization SSS and implement my plan in his village Allipur. He promised to spend up to Rs. 25 lakhs on my plan. I began working as CEO of SSS. I organized the organization’s documents and accounts management, prepared various project proposals, and submitted them to departments. I also produced a documentary in Delhi on development work for SSS. However, Dr. Trivedi never actually supported implementation of my plan financially. I continued working based on his promises.
In SSS with the support of AIESEC International I facilitated study research, conducted by students of 14 countries, on various village issues and helped in preparing development plans,.  
To train elected representatives of Gram Panchayats throughout Uttar Pradesh I prepared a proposal for 18 institutions, because it was initiative of the Government of India. But due to failure to follow ethical principles, the proposal was not approved as a unified initiative. Instead, different organizations secured separate approvals for different districts. SSS too received major assignments independently for building capacity of elected representatives.  
When the Anna movement against corruption began, I participated in a 14-day hunger strike demanding establishment of the Lokpal system. However, after one day of fasting, I realized that instead of sitting on strike it would be more meaningful for me to serve the other protestors. I began massaging people’s heads, arranging water and medicines, and ensuring medical treatment if anyone fell ill. After the agitation ended, I honored all participants on behalf of SSS.
I then formed a group and launched the “Honesty in Governance” movement from Kakori. Initially everyone stayed together, but later many became leaders themselves. Some went to Delhi and became associated with the Aam Aadmi Party. Sanjay too used to visit the office, hold press conferences in my room, and even stay overnight, but I never found discussions with him meaningful. Later I stopped talking to him. Dr. Trivedi greatly supported Sanjay and sometimes even visited Delhi to assist Arvind Kejriwal.
Because of my plan for Gram Swaraj, on 12 January 2014, twenty-one organizations honored me as an N.G.I. (Non-Government Institution), which was later registered as a trust and proved beneficial to many people and institutions. Yet no one made any real effort to implement my plan.
At a political gathering organized by Shri Piyush Shukla, I met Dr. Manjul Kant Dwivedi. I learned that he was an internationally known Vastu expert with followers even in America. His organization, International Society for Spiritual Sciences (ISSS), was working on meditation and spiritual energy. He appreciated both my knowledge and my development plan. Dr. Dwivedi was especially impressed by the documents I had prepared for SSS. Therefore, he requested me to help arrange international support for building an ashram for his organization and promised to support implementation of my development plan in a village near Rishikesh.
I went to Rishikesh. The place was excellent. The institution owned a hilltop site where there was a small temple containing an unbroken idol of the Goddess. Dr. Sahib asked me to meditate there and share my perception of the place’s energy. Through meditation I felt the place was ideal—suitable for meditation and teaching meditation. The place was in Kothar village, about three kilometers from Neelkanth. I liked both the village and the location. As an NGI, I began working to organize ISSS documents and accounts in a way suitable for receiving international assistance. At the same time, I also began helping in the construction of the ashram. In Rishikesh, I introduced Dr. Dwivedi in villages, schools, and markets as a spiritual master, and he soon became recognized internationally. Since ISSS required a CEO in its documents, I was appointed CEO of ISSS.
After working together for one and a half years, I realized that Dr. Dwivedi had the ability to use spiritual influence for personal interests. He wanted me to live permanently in the ashram, teach meditation, and work for the ashram’s development and publicity. This was not possible for me.
As an NGI, I prepared plans for SIRD related to studies of rural development work and also wrote study reports. Through SIRD, I got opportunities to work as a trainer in social audit programmes concerning rural development activities across the country.
To ensure transparency and accountability in rural development programmes, the Ministry of Rural Development (MoRD), Government of India, together with the Tata Institute of Social Sciences (TISS), implemented a programme to provide one-month training to Social Auditors appointed across the country. In this programme, six social workers from Uttar Pradesh, including me, were appointed as Lead Course Coordinators (LCCs), while six SIRD officers were appointed as National Level Monitors.
Like other states, we organized training programmes. I felt that I had finally found a “force” for implementing my ideas. Together we were supposed to accelerate development work in villages. It was decided that the Social Auditors would demonstrate participatory village development in one village of Unnao district. Auditors from Kanpur district even established a trust named Gram Swaraj Foundation and started work in a village in Unnao. However, they could not continue regularly, and I had to abandon the effort. I observed that the training had little effect on most social auditors. Most were still following prevailing corrupt practices. I became deeply discouraged and decided not to engage further in village development work.
However, on the encouragement of some experts committed to rural development, I made one final effort. Along with the former village head of Bari Garhi village in Malihabad, representatives of Self Help Groups, and a NREGA mate, we established a trust named Appropriate Institute of Rural Development (AIRD). It was registered on 31.01.2020 under the Public Charitable Act, 1882.
Without any external financial support, we worked intensively for three years in the villages of Malihabad and developed what we believe is an effective participatory process. We now wish to demonstrate this process in a village near the State Institute of Rural Development (D.D.U.S.I.R.D.) BkT. Lucknow.
